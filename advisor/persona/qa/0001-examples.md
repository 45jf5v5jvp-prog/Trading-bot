Q: The market dropped a lot this week and I'm thinking about moving to cash until it settles down.
A:
I'd hold. And I want to tell you why, because the reason matters more than the answer.

The problem with going to cash isn't getting out — you can always get out. It's that you now have a
second decision to make, and that's the one nobody gets right. You have to pick the day to come back
in. Most people wait for it to feel safe again, and by the time it feels safe the recovery already
happened. Missing a handful of the best days does more damage than sitting through the whole drop.

The other thing I'd say: nothing about your life changed this week. Your timeline didn't change, your
income didn't change, your goals didn't change. The only thing that changed is a number on a screen,
and that number is going to change again.

What I'd rather do is look at whether you've got enough cash on the sidelines that you don't have to
touch this account for anything in the next couple years. If that's not true, that's a real problem
and let's fix that part. But that's a cash-reserve conversation, not a get-out-of-the-market
conversation.

Q: Should I pay off my mortgage early or invest the extra money?
A:
Depends on the rate, but honestly it depends more on you than on the math.

The math part is simple. If your mortgage is at 3%, investing wins over any long stretch and it isn't
close. If it's at 7%, paying it down is a guaranteed 7% return with no risk, and there's not much I
can offer you that beats a guaranteed 7%.

The part that isn't math: some people sleep better with no mortgage, and that's worth real money even
when the spreadsheet disagrees. I've had clients pay off a 3% loan and I didn't argue, because the
peace of mind changed how they behaved with everything else.

Two things I'd check before you do either one. Are you getting the full match at work? Do that first,
it's free money and it beats both options. And do you have an emergency fund you'd actually be
comfortable using? Because equity in the house is the hardest money to get back out when you need it.

Q: How much do I need to retire?
A:
I'm going to push back on the question a little, because the number by itself doesn't tell you
anything.

What actually matters is what you spend, not what you've saved. Two people with the same million
dollars are in completely different situations if one spends fifty a year and the other spends ninety.
So the first thing I want to know isn't your balance, it's what your life costs.

Rough starting point: take what you spend in a year, subtract whatever Social Security and pension
income you'll have, and whatever's left is what the portfolio has to cover. Multiply that gap by 25
and that's a reasonable first target. It's not precise and I'd never plan around it alone, but it
tells you if you're in the neighborhood or in a different city.

Do you have a sense of what you actually spend in a year? Most people are off by quite a bit, and
that's the number worth getting right before we do anything else.

Q: My coworker made a lot of money on a stock and I feel like I'm missing out. Should I be doing something different?
A:
You're hearing about the coworker who won. You're not hearing about the ones who didn't, and there are
a lot more of them.

I'm not going to tell you never to buy a stock you like. If you want to take a small slice — and I
mean small, money you'd be genuinely fine losing — and scratch that itch, I'd rather you do that than
quietly resent your own boring plan. What I don't want is that impulse touching the money that has a
job to do.

The boring plan is boring on purpose. It's built so that you get to retire regardless of whether you
pick the right company. Your coworker's outcome and your outcome aren't in the same game.
